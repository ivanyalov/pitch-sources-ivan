# Pitch methods, mistakes, and examples

Curated on 22 October 2026 for a shared early-stage startup pitch playbook.

## Purpose and selection

Working format: a startup pitch of approximately 15 minutes, followed by a separate hardware demonstration. The duration remains provisional. The team has no funding or other audience-resource request.

The presentation should explain a customer need, the evidence behind it, the proposed value, and a plausible business. Hardware choices should follow from user requirements. Distinguish the end user from the buyer when those roles differ.

This list prioritizes primary sources: framework owners, investors writing about their own practice, and founders publishing their own decks. Resource descriptions summarize published text. Video entries are recommendations based on their publishers' descriptions; they do not imply that the full videos have been watched or independently evaluated. Suggested applications and study questions are editorial recommendations for this project.

## Suggested starting sequence

1. YC: How to Pitch Your Company — formulate clear answers.
2. Sequoia: Writing a Business Plan — check business coverage.
3. IDEO.org: Frame Your Design Challenge — keep the problem open to different solutions.
4. YC: How to Design a Better Pitch Deck — translate the argument into readable slides.
5. Reid Hoffman's annotated LinkedIn deck — examine how a founder supports a thesis and discusses mistakes.

Then use NABC to review the value proposition, and DARPA's questions to define what the hardware demonstration should establish.

## A. Frameworks and preparation

### 1. Y Combinator — How to Pitch Your Company

Michael Seibel. Article.

[Read the original](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-pitch-your-company/)

Seven questions covering what the company does, market, progress, unique insight, business model, team, and what the presenter wants. Useful for replacing vague startup language with simple explanations.

Project exercise: write a short answer to each relevant question. Describe the research insight specifically, and state what has been achieved in what period. Omit an audience request because none exists.

### 2. Sequoia — Writing a Business Plan

Team Sequoia. Short guide.

[Read the original](https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan)

Covers purpose, customer problem and current workarounds, solution, timing, market, alternatives, business model, team, financials if available, and vision. The published list does not prescribe a funding-ask section.

Project use: the main completeness checklist for the startup pitch. Financial assumptions should be identified as assumptions; early discovery does not establish sales or retention.

**Companion slide template — added at the team's request:** [Sequoia Capital Pitch Deck Template on SlideShare](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/sequoia-capital-pitchdecktemplate/46231251).

Attribution: uploaded by Malcolm Lewis; the cover identifies it as a reproduction by PitchDeckCoach based on Sequoia's guidance. Keep it alongside the official article as a practical slide template, with that provenance recorded.

The template includes an explicit Product section covering form factor, functionality, architecture, intellectual property, and a development roadmap. Its Solution section asks for use cases and where the product physically sits. These prompts are useful for connecting the startup story to the separate hardware demonstration.

Project adaptation: use the content prompts without treating every section as mandatory. The financial/deal material should fit the team's stage and absence of a funding request. The template's flow overview is explicitly marked as planning guidance rather than a slide to include in the presentation.

### 3. IDEO.org — Create a Pitch

Design Kit. Method page.

[Read the original](https://www.designkit.org/methods/create-a-pitch.html)

Explain the essence of the idea, its significance, how it differs, and who benefits; adapt detail and length to the audience. This is communication guidance, rather than a prescribed slide-by-slide startup deck.

Project exercise: describe the idea in under a minute, keeping the value for parent and purchaser understandable.

### 4. IDEO.org — Frame Your Design Challenge

Design Kit. Method page.

[Read the original](https://www.designkit.org/methods/frame-your-design-challenge.html)

Frame a challenge with an appropriate scope, intended impact, real constraints, and room for multiple possible solutions.

Project exercise: check whether the problem statement allows a non-robot solution. Explain why the chosen physical device is appropriate after establishing the need.

Companion method: [Define Your Audience](https://www.designkit.org/methods/define-your-audience.html), for the surrounding ecosystem and influences on adoption.

### 5. NABC — Need, Approach, Benefits per costs, Competition and alternatives

Carlson Value Creation Initiative, Worcester Polytechnic Institute. Framework reference and application article.

[Framework reference](https://www.wpi.edu/about/value-creation-initiative) · [Application: Answering the Million-Dollar Question, Part 2](https://www.wpi.edu/news/answering-million-dollar-question-part-2)

Use four questions to explain a value proposition: the important need, the approach, the value relative to costs, and the comparison with competing approaches. The application article discusses translating technical work into value that a reviewer can assess.

Project exercise: compare the proposed experience with calls, cameras, speakers, wearables, and other relevant arrangements. Consider user effort and maintenance alongside money. This application to family care is our adaptation.

Download note: the [library](../library/README.md) includes WPI's value-proposition slides and its two-page NABC reference card.

### 6. Duarte — The Ultimate Guide to Contrast: What Your Presentation Is Missing

Phoebe Perelman / Duarte. Article.

[Read the original](https://www.duarte.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-contrast/)

Explains narrative, visual, and delivery contrast, including the movement between the present situation and a possible future.

Project exercise: describe one research-grounded family situation today and the intended improvement with the product. Clearly distinguish intended outcomes from observed results.

### 7. a16z — The Insider's Guide to Data Rooms: What to Know Before You Raise

Justine Moore. Article. Supporting reference rather than the first read.

[Read the original](https://a16z.com/the-insiders-guide-to-data-rooms-what-to-know-before-you-raise/)

Includes minimum pitch-deck content and discusses supporting evidence. Its treatment of detailed financial projections concerns early consumer-company data rooms, not a universal rule for every deck.

Project use: ask what evidence stands behind each business claim. The full fundraising paperwork is outside this assignment's purpose.

### 8. DARPA — The Heilmeier Catechism

Official research-program assessment questions. Reference page.

[Read the original](https://www.darpa.mil/about/heilmeier-catechism)

Questions cover the objective, current practice and limitations, new approach, significance, risks, cost, time, and tests of success.

Project exercise: specify what the hardware demo establishes, how success will be observed, and which uncertainties remain. This is an engineering-review aid, not a startup-deck template.

## B. Mistakes and failure modes

### 9. Y Combinator — How to Design a Better Pitch Deck

Kevin Hale. Illustrated article with before-and-after slides.

[Read the original](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck/)

Study illegible text, excessive detail, unclear visual hierarchy, and slides that require the audience to infer the point. The article contains concrete revisions, including startup-description slides.

Adaptation: its short-deck recommendations are written for a roughly 2.5-minute Demo Day slot. Transfer the clarity principles to the provisional 15-minute pitch, rather than copying the slide count.

### 10. Y Combinator — A Guide to Demo Day Presentations

Geoff Ralston. Article.

[Read the original](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/)

Useful on the few messages the audience should retain, rehearsal, delivery, and common mistakes. Covers delayed explanations of the product, overstated claims, complicated slides, and cumulative growth charts.

Adaptation: advice about avoiding demos and video belongs to the short investor-pitch context. Your hardware demonstration is a separate required part; use the article's communication principles without removing it.

### 11. Hustle Fund / Hustle Commons — Pitch Deck Mistakes series

Kera Demars. Five short articles.

- [Part 1: length and unnecessary detail](https://www.hustlecommons.com/blog/part-1-pitch-deck-mistakes)
- [Part 2: the problem slide](https://www.hustlecommons.com/blog/part-2-pitch-deck-mistakes)
- [Part 3: differentiation](https://www.hustlecommons.com/blog/part-3-pitch-deck-mistakes)
- [Part 4: scalability](https://www.hustlecommons.com/blog/part-4-pitch-deck-mistakes)
- [Part 5: product focus](https://www.hustlecommons.com/blog/part-5-pitch-deck-mistakes)

Project exercise: audit whether the need is evident, the distinction matters to the customer, and the product story stays focused.

Adaptation: the series explicitly reflects a pre-seed investor's preferences. Its deck-length and fundraising prescriptions are context-specific.

### 12. a16z — “Need More Time” or Lack of Product-Market Fit?

Martin Casado. Article on early markets; especially relevant to technology-led teams.

[Read the original](https://a16z.com/need-more-time-or-lack-of-product-market-fit-guideposts-for-tech-founders-going-to-market-when-no-market-exists/)

Discusses misleading expressions of interest, adoption barriers, and competition with the status quo. Section 3 directly addresses artificial competitive-quadrant slides.

Project exercise: compare against what the family actually uses. Treat enthusiasm about a prototype separately from commitment to buy and continued use. The original examples concern enterprise technology; the family-care application is our inference.

## C. Examples to inspect

### 13. LinkedIn — Series B pitch with Reid Hoffman's annotations

Founder-published deck and extensive commentary.

[Open the deck and annotations](https://www.reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/)

The author explains the original context, the thesis, and changes he would make. He explicitly acknowledges stylistic and substantive errors.

Study question: which belief does each slide support? Pay particular attention to objections, analogy, and his discussion of multiple revenue streams. Use it as an annotated argument rather than a visual style template.

### 14. Intercom — First pitch deck

Eoghan McCabe. Founder-published article linking the late-2011 deck.

[Open the founder's post and embedded deck](https://www.intercom.com/blog/first-pitch-deck)

Study question: how quickly can an unfamiliar reader identify the customer, problem, product, and ambition? Compare the scope of an early pitch with a mature company's current product.

The deck is embedded through SlideShare; use the embedded-media link if it does not appear inline.

### 15. a16z Pitch Room — Sandbox VR

Video, 48:09. Founders Steve Zhao and Siqi Chen, with investor Andrew Chen.

[Open the pitch and investor commentary](https://a16z.com/the-a16z-pitch-room-sandbox-vr/)

The publisher describes a lightly edited pitch followed by a slide-by-slide investor discussion of what resonated and what did not.

Study question: how does a technology-intensive physical experience become an understandable customer and business proposition? Useful to compare with your pitch-to-hardware transition; it is not a care-robot example.

### 16. Nancy Duarte — The Secret Structure of Great Talks

TEDx talk, with analysis of Steve Jobs' iPhone launch and Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech.

[Open the talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/nancy_duarte_the_secret_structure_of_great_talks)

Study question: how do explanation, contrast, demonstration, and pacing support an idea? Focus on the product-launch analysis for the hardware presentation. A launch keynote has a different purpose from an early-stage startup pitch.

## How to review examples as a team

For each example, record:

1. What is the central claim?
2. Who is the user, who pays, and what decision is the audience being invited to make?
3. What evidence supports the problem and the proposed value?
4. What current alternative is being displaced or supplemented?
5. What is demonstrated, what is already validated, and what remains a future claim?
6. What should we borrow, and what depends on the original company's stage or audience?

Judge those elements separately from the company's later success or the amount it raised.
